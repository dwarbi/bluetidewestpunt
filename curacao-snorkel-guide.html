Curaçao Snorkel Guide

What's Down There in Curaçao

Sea turtles, reef squid, octopus, eels and barracuda — what you're likely to see snorkeling around the island, and where to go looking.

The Headliners

What people come to see

Curaçao's reefs start right off the beach at a lot of spots, so you don't need a boat or a dive certificate to see most of this. None of it is guaranteed on any single swim, but snorkel a few different beaches over a few days and you'll tick off a lot of this list.

Look, don't touch anything. Coral, fish, turtles, all of it. Hands off keeps the reef intact and saves you from stings, scrapes and spines too.
Reef-safe sunscreen only. Regular sunscreen is toxic to coral. Mineral (zinc oxide) is the way to go.
Photo: Geoff Shuetrim, CC BY
01

Green Sea Turtle

Chelonia mydas
Top sighting

The one everyone's hoping for, and in Curaçao there are a lot of them. Snorkel a few beaches and you'll very likely see one. Greens have a short, rounded snout and a smooth shell, and they surface every few minutes for air. They're endangered and protected — hang back a few body lengths, never touch or chase, and let them come and go on their own terms.

Photo: Kyle Van Houtan, CC BY
02

Hawksbill Sea Turtle

Eretmochelys imbricata
Critically endangered

Easy to mix up with a green, so here's the tell: a hawksbill has a narrow, pointed, bird-like beak and a shell of overlapping plates with jagged back edges. They stick close to the reef, picking at sponges, and you'll see plenty of them around the island. They're critically endangered, so give them extra space.

Photo: Pauline Walsh Jacobson, CC BY 4.0
03

Porcupinefish

Diodon hystrix
Big and curious

A slow, round-bodied fish covered in short spines that lie flat unless it feels threatened. They get big, up to around 18 inches, and genuinely curious — give one space and it'll often drift closer to check you out. Usually hovering near rocks or ledges during the day.

Photo: Dan Schofield, CC BY
04

Caribbean Reef Squid

Sepioteuthis sepioidea
Curious and colorful

Usually in small groups hovering in a neat line near the surface, and often just as curious about you as you are about them. Stay still and they'll hold their ground, flashing through colors and patterns — that's how they talk to each other. Sheltered coves and cliff walls are good places to look.

Photo: Denis Hurley
05

Brazilian Reef Octopus

Octopus insularis
Look for the den

This is the octopus you'll find around Curaçao, and unlike a lot of octopuses it's often out hunting during the day. Look for a small hole with a little pile of empty shells outside it — that's a den, and there may be an eye watching you. Out in the open it changes color and texture almost instantly, from reddish-brown to pale and bumpy in a blink.

Photo: Don Loarie, CC BY
06

Great Barracuda

Sphyraena barracuda
Watchful, keeps its distance

Long, silver, and toothy, usually hanging nearly motionless a little way off, keeping an eye on things. They mostly keep to themselves and aren't interested in snorkelers, but give them their space. Leave shiny jewelry on shore, since a flash of metal can look like a baitfish.

07

Eels

Morays and more
How many kinds can you spot?
Photo: Dan Schofield, CC BY
Green MorayGymnothorax funebris
The big one. Can grow well over five feet, and we've seen one that size. Usually just a head and a slowly opening mouth poking out of the reef.
Photo: Jay Brasher, CC BY
Spotted MorayGymnothorax moringa
The one you're most likely to see — white-ish with dark spots, peeking out of a hole. Often out hunting in the open in the early morning or evening.
Photo: Jacob Weston, CC BY
Sharptail EelMyrichthys breviceps
Not actually a moray. Slender and spotted, and unlike the morays it swims right out in the open, poking its head into the sand and rubble looking for food.

There are lots of kinds of eels around the island, and once you start noticing them you'll spot them everywhere. The mouth opening and closing isn't a threat — that's just how they breathe. We've also seen banded ones, and chain and goldentail morays live around the island too. Keep your hands out of holes and crevices.

Photo: desertnaturalist, CC BY 4.0
08

Queen Angelfish

Holacanthus ciliaris
Showstopper

Gold body, blue-tipped fins, and a dark "crown" spot ringed in electric blue on the forehead, which is where the name comes from. Solitary and a little shy, but worth the extra few minutes of quiet drifting to find.

Photo: keesgroenendijk, CC BY
09

Trumpetfish

Aulostomus maculatus
Hangs at odd angles

Long and skinny, and usually just hanging there tilted sideways or head-down, like it forgot which way is up. Often tucked in among sea rods and soft coral, lined up with the branches. They'll also shadow a bigger fish, using it as cover to sneak up on prey. Brown, yellow, or bluish-grey — they change color to blend in, so look for the long snout.

Photo: desertnaturalist, CC BY 4.0
10

Stoplight Parrotfish

Sparisoma viride
Listen for the crunch

Big, colorful, and doing the reef a real favor: parrotfish scrape algae off coral with a beak-like jaw, and much of the white sand on Curaçao's beaches ultimately comes out the back end of fish like these. Listen near the rocks and you can actually hear them crunching.

Photo: Eamon Corbett, CC BY
11

Peacock Flounder

Bothus lunatus
Can you spot it?

A flat fish lying on the sand with both eyes on one side of its head, covered in bright blue rings. It can change color to match the bottom almost perfectly, so you'll often see one only when it moves. Scan the sandy patches between reef sections.

Photo: Chris Taklis, CC BY
12

Flying Gurnard

Dactylopterus volitans
Watch it open its wings

Brownish and easy to overlook as it "walks" along the sand on little finger-like fins. Get too close and it spreads a pair of huge fan-shaped fins edged in electric blue, like wings. Look over sandy patches in shallow water, and move slowly.

Photo: Matteo Bellucci, CC BY
13

Spotted Drum

Equetus punctatus
Look under ledges

The juveniles are the prize: small, black and white, with a ridiculously long trailing fin on top that they wave around as they swim in little loops. Adults are striped and spotted, with a tall fin of their own. They tuck under ledges and into crevices, and tend to stick to the same spot, so once you find one you can usually go back to it.

Photo: Rachel Crane, CC BY
14

Fairy Basslet

Gramma loreto · also called royal gramma
Often upside down

A tiny fish, only a couple of inches long, that's bright purple in front and bright yellow in back. Look under ledges and overhangs, where they hover close to the rock, often swimming upside down along the ceiling. Small, but the colors are hard to beat.

Photo: keesgroenendijk, CC BY
15

Caribbean Spiny Lobster

Panulirus argus
Look for the antennae

Usually the first thing you see is a pair of long, spiny antennae poking out from under a ledge or crevice, waving slowly. Hang still and you may get a look at the rest: a big, spiny, reddish-brown body with pale spots. No claws, unlike the lobsters most people picture.

Photo: Robin White, CC BY
16

Filefish

Cantherhines macrocerus, Aluterus scriptus & others
Odd little shapes

A few kinds turn up around the island. The whitespotted filefish can switch between orange covered in white spots and plain grey in seconds; the scrawled filefish is long and flat, covered in blue scribbles, and often swims at a strange tilted angle. Usually in pairs.

Photo: Jay Brasher, CC BY
17

Honeycomb Cowfish

Acanthostracion polygonius
Look for the horns

A boxy little fish with two short "horns" sticking out over its eyes and a hexagon pattern all over its body, like a honeycomb. It's a close cousin of the trunkfish and moves the same slow, hovering way, so if you find one you can usually get a good look. Can shift its colors and pattern to blend in.

Photo: Daniel Benák, CC BY
18

Lionfish

Pterois volitans / miles
Invasive — don't touch

Beautiful and a real problem: lionfish aren't native to the Caribbean and eat huge numbers of young reef fish. Curaçao fights back with divers culling them, and they show up on local restaurant menus — order one. Their spines are venomous, so look but keep your distance.

Photo: Denis Hurley
19

Elkhorn Coral

Acropora palmata
Critically endangered

Named for its wide, flattened branches that really do look like antlers. Once the dominant reef-building coral across the Caribbean, it's now critically endangered after mass die-offs — what's left is precious. Never touch it, stand on it, or let a fin brush it.

The Everyday Reef

What you'll see on almost every swim

Less famous, but these are the fish you'll actually be looking at most of the time — and the ones people most often want to put a name to.

Photo: desertnaturalist, CC BY 4.0

Sergeant Major

The welcoming committee — yellow-and-black stripes, and they'll check you out within seconds.

Photo: Denis Hurley

Blue Tang

Electric blue discs moving in loose groups over the reef. Juveniles are bright yellow.

Photo: Denis Hurley

Bluehead Wrasse

One of the most common fish anywhere. Only the adult males have the blue head.

Photo: desertnaturalist, CC BY 4.0

French Angelfish

Black with gold-edged scales that sparkle as it turns. Often in pairs.

Photo: Paul Asman and Jill Lenoble, CC BY 2.0

Yellowtail Snapper

Silvery with a bold yellow stripe into a yellow tail, often in schools.

Photo: desertnaturalist, CC BY 4.0

Foureye Butterflyfish

A fake eye near the tail confuses predators. Almost always in pairs.

Photo: desertnaturalist, CC BY 4.0

Spanish Hogfish

Purple front, yellow back, and works as a cleaner fish for others.

Photo: Dan Schofield, CC BY 4.0

Smooth Trunkfish

A polka-dotted box with fins. Slow and easy to get close to.

Photo: Nick Hobgood, CC BY-SA 3.0

Christmas Tree Worms

Tiny colorful spirals on coral that vanish if you wave a hand nearby.

Photo: Dan Schofield, CC BY

Flamingo Tongue

A small snail, usually crawling on sea fans. The orange-and-black spotted pattern isn't its shell but living tissue wrapped over it, so please don't pick it up.

Photo: keesgroenendijk, CC BY

Yellow Goatfish

Silvery with a yellow stripe, in groups over the sand, digging with two little "whiskers" under the chin.

Photo: Pauline Walsh Jacobson, CC BY 4.0

Purple Sea Fans & Soft Coral

The swaying purple fans and sea rods that give the reef its texture.

Rare Sightings

If you're lucky

These are around, but don't plan your trip on them. Spot one and you've got a story.

Photo: Dan Schofield, CC BY

Spotted Eagle Ray

Aetobatus narinari

Dark and covered in white spots, "flying" by with slow wingbeats and a long tail. Usually cruising along the edge of deeper water, so keep an eye on the blue.

Photo: Pauline Walsh Jacobson, CC BY

Seahorse

Hippocampus spp.

Very rare, but they're there. Tiny and wrapped around a sea rod or piece of debris, and nearly impossible to spot unless you go slow.

Where: Tugboat Beach
Photo: Austen Novis, CC BY

Frogfish

Antennarius multiocellatus (longlure frogfish)

A lumpy, sponge-looking ambush fish that barely moves. Divers hunt for them with guides — if you spot one snorkeling, you've done something special.

Be Careful

Four to watch out for

The reef is a safe place to snorkel as long as you keep your hands to yourself and watch where you put your feet. These four are the ones to be especially careful around.

Photo: Roger A. Morales-Flores, CC BY

Fire Coral

Millepora spp.

Mustard-yellow to brown, growing in blades or branches with pale tips, and often encrusting other things so it doesn't look like much. Brushing against it leaves a stinging, itchy burn. Easy to avoid once you know what it looks like.

Photo: Denis Hurley

Long-Spined Sea Urchin

Diadema antillarum

Black and spiky, wedged into rock crevices in shallow water, especially around rocky entry points. The spines break off in skin and are no fun to remove. Water shoes help, and watch where you put your feet.

Photo: Jay Brasher, CC BY

Spotted Scorpionfish

Scorpaena plumieri

Mottled brown, lumpy and completely still, so it looks like part of the rock or rubble it's sitting on. The spines on its back are venomous and very painful. The risk is putting a hand or foot down on one without seeing it, which is one more reason not to touch or stand on anything.

Photo: A H, CC BY

Bearded Fireworm

Hermodice carunculata

A fuzzy, segmented worm, orange to greenish with tufts of white bristles down its sides, crawling out in the open over coral and rock. It looks harmless, but the bristles break off in skin and burn for hours. Look, but don't poke.

Anemones and some sponges can also sting or irritate skin, which is one more reason to keep your hands to yourself.

Where To Go

A few beaches to start with

There are countless good snorkel spots on the island. These six are spread around it and make a good place to start. Blue Tide Westpunt is on the west coast, a short drive from some of the best of them: Playa Kalki, Playa Lagun, Cas Abou and Daaibooi.

Westpunt · far west

Playa Kalki

Known to divers as "Alice in Wonderland" for its big coral formations, and the reef starts close to shore.

Look for: big coral, angelfish

Northwest coast

Playa Lagun

A narrow, sheltered cove with calm water and cliff walls on both sides. Good for beginners.

Look for: reef squid, turtles

Central west coast

Cas Abou

Full facilities and very clear water. Swim to the northern side for the most active reef.

Look for: everyday reef fish, parrotfish

Central west coast

Daaibooi

A local favorite. Follow the rocky cliffs on either side of the bay.

Look for: turtles

Southeast · Caracasbaai

Tugboat Beach

A small sunken tugboat sits in shallow water, covered in coral and fish. Easy to reach from shore, and the one spot where we've heard of seahorses.

Look for: the wreck, seahorses (rare)

Offshore · by boat

Klein Curaçao

A small uninhabited island off the southeast tip, with a white-sand beach, an old lighthouse and a shipwreck up on the shore. You get there on a day trip by boat. Turtles are regulars. Currents can be strong on the far side, so stay where your crew tells you.

Look for: turtles, clear open water

Before You Get In

A Few Things Worth Knowing

Curaçao's reefs are in good shape. Help keep them that way.

Reef-safe sunscreen only

Oxybenzone and octinoxate are toxic to coral even in tiny amounts. Go mineral-based (zinc oxide), or wear a rash guard and skip most of the sunscreen.

Don't feed, chase, or touch

That goes double for turtles. Feeding changes how fish behave, and a single touch or fin kick can undo years of coral growth.

Bring your own gear & water shoes

Many of the best spots have rocky entries and no rental stand. Your own mask and fins fit better, and water shoes save you from urchins and sharp rock.

Try iNaturalist

A free app and a huge community of naturalists. Snap a photo while you snorkel and it helps you figure out what you saw.

Photo: Dan Schofield, CC BY
Extremely Rare

And once in a very long while… a shark

Very rarely, a shark shows up — and it may even be a hammerhead. It's not something to expect or worry about, just a reminder that the open ocean starts right off these reefs.
