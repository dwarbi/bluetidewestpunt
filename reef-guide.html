Sergeant Major

Abudefduf saxatilis

The welcoming committee. These yellow-and-black striped damselfish patrol the shallows in loose schools and will investigate you within seconds of getting in. Territorial around the rocks where they guard purple egg patches — give a nesting one a little berth and it'll leave you alone.

Stoplight Parrotfish

Sparisoma viride

Big, unmistakable, and doing the reef a real favor: parrotfish graze on algae by scraping coral with a fused beak-like jaw, which is where all that white sand on the beach ultimately comes from. Listen closely underwater near the rocks and you can actually hear them crunching.

Easy to spot Up to 20 in. Near coral heads

Blue Tang

Acanthurus coeruleus

Electric blue, disc-shaped, and usually moving in loose groups over the reef flat. Look for a small orange-yellow scalpel-like spine near the base of the tail — it's how the fish defends itself, and also how it got its "surgeonfish" family name. Juveniles are bright yellow before they turn blue.

Reef flats Grazer

Foureye Butterflyfish

Chaetodon capistratus

A pale, disc-shaped fish with a bold black eyespot near the tail and a real eye camouflaged in a dark stripe — a decoy that confuses predators about which end is the head. Usually seen in pairs; they mate for life and stay close to their partner across the reef.

Queen Angelfish

Holacanthus ciliaris

The showiest fish on this reef — gold body, blue-tipped fins, and a dark "crown" spot ringed in electric blue on the forehead, which is where the name comes from. Solitary and a little shy, but worth the extra few minutes of quiet drifting to find.

Yellowtail Snapper

Ocyurus chrysurus

Silvery, fast, and marked by a bold yellow stripe running the length of the body straight into a matching yellow tail. Often in schools mid-water rather than hugging the bottom — if you see a shimmering group moving as one, it's probably these.

French Angelfish

Pomacanthus paru

Deep black body edged in gold scales that catch the light like sequins as it turns. Juveniles look completely different — black with vertical yellow stripes — so don't be surprised if a "different species" near a coral head turns out to be a young French angel.

Bar Jack

Carangoides ruber

Sleek, silvery, and fast — a dark stripe runs along the back down into an electric-blue tail. Bar jacks often shadow larger fish or even snorkelers, using the disturbance to snap up anything small stirred off the bottom. Don't be surprised if one starts tailing you.

Christmas Tree Worms

Spirobranchus giganteus

Tiny, brilliant, and everywhere once you know to look — twin spiral "trees" in electric blue, orange, or yellow, no bigger than a fingertip, growing straight out of brain and boulder coral. Wave a hand nearby (without touching the coral) and watch them vanish instantly into their tube.

Purple Sea Fans & Soft Coral

Gorgonia ventalina, and relatives

Not animals you'll see moving, but the reef's architecture — tall purple fans and swaying "sea whips" that sway with the surge and give the whole reef its texture. They're delicate; a single fin kick can snap decades of growth, so keep a body-length of distance and check your fin position before you stand up.

Sun Anemone

Stichodactyla helianthus

There's a big cluster of these right at our spot — a flat, carpet-like anemone in green and gold with hundreds of short, thick tentacles. Sun anemones pack a real sting (worse than most Caribbean anemones), so admire without touching. Look closely between the tentacles and you'll usually spot the little crabs that live there.

Long-Spined Sea Urchin

Diadema antillarum

The one thing on this list that can genuinely ruin your afternoon if you're careless. Black, spiky, and usually wedged into rock crevices in shallower water near the entry ladder — easy to see and easy to avoid, but the spines break off in skin and are no fun to remove. Watch your footing at entry and exit.

Watch your step Near rocks / ladder

Banded Clinging Crab

Mithraculus cinctimanus

These are the little crabs living in the sun anemone cluster — small, olive and cream, with pale-banded claws. It's a genuine partnership: the crab gets protection in the anemone's stinging tentacles, and it helps keep the anemone clean in return. Look closely between the tentacles rather than reaching in.

Blue Chromis

Chromis cyanea

The little iridescent blue fish that hover in loose clouds just above the coral. They pick plankton straight out of the water column and scatter into the reef the instant anything comes close, only to regroup a few seconds later. One of the most common sights on the whole reef.

Redlip Blenny

Ophioblennius macclurei

A small, dark, torpedo-shaped fish that perches on rocks and dead coral rather than swimming freely, darting a few inches at a time. Look for the bright red-orange lips that give it its name — easiest to spot when it's sitting still, propped up on its pectoral fins.

Orange Cup Coral

Tubastraea coccinea

Small clusters of bright orange polyps tucked into shaded crevices and overhangs rather than out in the open. Unlike reef-building corals, these don't rely on sunlight — look for them under ledges and ridges where the light doesn't reach directly.

Shaded crevices Bright orange

Elkhorn Coral

Acropora palmata

Named for its wide, flattened branches that really do resemble elk antlers. This was once the dominant reef-building coral across the entire Caribbean, and it's now critically endangered after mass die-offs — what's left is precious. Never touch it, stand on it, or let a fin brush against it; a single contact can undo decades of growth.

Tidal Spray Crab

Plagusia depressa

Reddish-brown and very fast, living right at the waterline on rocks rather than underwater proper — you'll likely spot this one getting in or out at the ladder rather than while snorkeling. Freezes at the first sign of movement, then bolts sideways into a crevice.

Touch-Me-Not Sponge

Neofibularia nolitangere

An olive-green, lumpy encrusting sponge covering the rock in irregular patches, dotted with small round pores. It's not coral and it's not soft the way it might look — contact with skin can cause an itchy, uncomfortable rash that lingers for a day or two. Easy to avoid once you know what it looks like.

Porcupinefish

Diodon hystrix

A slow, round-bodied fish covered in short spines that lie flat unless it feels threatened — it can inflate into a spiky ball as a last resort, but that's a stress response, not something to try to provoke. Usually seen hovering calmly near rocks or crevices during the day.

Bluehead Wrasse

Thalassoma bifasciatum

One of the most abundant fish on any Caribbean reef, constantly darting around in short bursts. Adult males have the namesake electric-blue head with black-and-white bands behind it; females and juveniles are plain yellow, so what looks like two different fish is often the same species.

Extremely common Constant quick movement
22 / 25

Smooth Trunkfish

Lactophrys triqueter

A boxy, slow-moving fish with a rigid, polka-dotted body armor instead of scales — it swims almost entirely with its small fins, giving it a distinctive hovering, deliberate motion unlike anything else on the reef. Easy to approach slowly since it rarely darts away.

Slow, boxy swimmer Easy to approach
23 / 25

Purple Stovepipe Sponge

Aplysina archeri

Tall, narrow purple tubes rising straight up off the reef in small clusters, sometimes reaching several feet — genuinely one of the more striking sights once you spot a good cluster. Look but don't grab; sponges are fragile and slow-growing.

Striking once you see it Fragile, slow-growing

Spanish Hogfish

Bodianus rufus

Two-toned and easy to identify — deep purple-violet on the front half, bright yellow on the back half, with a clean line between the two. Acts as a cleaner fish, picking parasites off other fish at informal "cleaning stations" on the reef.

Two-toned, easy ID Cleaner fish

Sharpnose Puffer

Canthigaster rostrata

Small, round, and easy to miss at first glance — look for the pointed snout and fine blue squiggly lines near the tail. Usually solitary, hovering close to the reef rather than swimming in open water, and generally unbothered by snorkelers nearby.

Before you get in

A healthy reef is the whole point

Reef-safe sunscreen only

Oxybenzone and octinoxate damage coral even in small amounts. Mineral-based (zinc oxide) sunscreen is what we stock in the unit.

Look, don't touch

Coral is a living animal and takes years to grow back from a single touch or fin strike. Keep hands and fins off everything, including the rocks — and give the sun anemones and touch-me-not sponge extra distance, since both can sting or irritate skin.

Don't feed or chase

Feeding fish changes their behavior and can make them sick. Give the elkhorn coral and any anemones a wide berth — one is critically endangered, the other stings.

Watch the entry ladder

Water shoes help on the rocks, and give the ladder area a glance for urchins and tidal spray crabs before you step down, especially at lower tide.
